### scheduling policy in action
1.Consider two processes : a text editor and a video encoder.
2.The text editor id I/O bound as it spends nearly all its time waiting for the user key presses.
3.The video encoder spends all its time applying video codec to the raw data.
4.The scheduler gives the text editor a larger proportion of the available processor than the video encoder, since it is interactive.
5.This is not because the text editor needs a lot of processing power, but because we want it to always have the processor time available the moment it needs it.
6.On OS's, this is done by giving the text editor a higher priority and larger timeslice than the video encoder by detecting the text editor is interactive.
7.in LINUX, If the video encoder and text editor are the only running processes and both are at the same nice level, this proportion would be 50%—each would be guaranteed half of the processor’s time.
8.since the text editor spends most of its time waiting for user input, i.e. key press, it does not use anywhere near 50% of the processor. Conversely, the video encoder is allowed to use more than its allotted 50%, to finish its execution quickly.

NOTE : BY 100% PROCESSOR TIME, IT MEANS TIME IT HAS AVAIABLE TO DISTRIBUTE, among the total distributable time available.

### what happens when the text editor wakes up
1.CFS notes that it is allotted 50% of the processor but has used considerably less.
2.Specifically, CFS determines that the text editor has run for less time than the video encoder.
3.Attempting to give all processes a fair share of the processor, it then preempts the video encoder and enables the text editor to run.
4.The text editor runs, quickly processes the
user’s key press, and again sleeps, waiting for more input.
